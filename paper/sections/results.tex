The study population included 67,845 patients with stroke registered in the
Norwegian Stroke Registry between 2014 and 2023, of whom 12,612 \((18.6\%\))
had uncertain-onset stroke (table \ref{tab:baseline}). Most patient
characteristics were similar between patients with uncertain-onset stroke and
all other strokes, with standardized mean differences generally below 0.10. The
largest differences were observed for living situation, and thrombolysis, while
differences in demographic characteristics, stroke type, and cardiovascular
history were modest. Living alone was more common among patients with
uncertain-onset stroke (45.2\% vs 37.2\%), and they recieved thrombolysis less
frequently (1.3\% vs 22.0\%).

\subsection*{Crude temporal and geographic variation in uncertain-onset stroke}

The crude proportion of uncertain-onset stroke varied over time, with the
heigest proportion in 2014 (27.9\%) and the lowest in 2020 (14.0\%). The
proportion declined during the early years of the study period, with a marked
reduciton between 2017 and 2018. It remained relatively low through 2020,
followed by a gradual increase to 18.5\% in 2023 (figure
\ref{fig:desc_temp_geo_var}A).

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \caption{Temporal and geographic variation in uncertain-onset stroke. A) Annual proportion of patients with uncertain-onset stroke, 2014–2023. B) Municipality-level geographic variation in uncertain-onset stroke.}
  \label{fig:desc_temp_geo_var}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/fig_2.png}
\end{figure}

In addition to the temporal variation, substantial geographic variation in
uncertain-onset stroke was observed across municipalities, ranging from 0\% to
62\% with a median of 16.7\% (IQR 10.2\% and 25.8\%) (Figure
\ref{fig:desc_temp_geo_var}B). Areas with relatively high and low proportions
appeared to cluster together geographically rather than an even distribution
across the country.

\subsection*{Patient and provider factors and geographic variation}

The base model only included year and municipality-level spatial effects. The
municipality-level effects displayed a similar geographic pattern as the crude
analysis, with the municipality-level estimates smoothed towards the mean
(Figure \ref{fig:adjusted_maps}A). The temporal pattern was also similar to the
crude analysis, with the odds of uncertain-onset stroke being lower in all
years compared to 2014 (supplementary table). The standard deviation of the
municipality effect of teh base model was 0.696 (95\% CrI 0.614--0.781), and
the spatial mixing parameter \(\phi\) was 0.976 (95\% CrI 0.969--0.982),
indicating that most of the geographic variation was spatially structured
(table \ref{tab:spatial_models}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig_3.png}
  \caption{Geographic variation showen as the municipality-level estimates of uncertain-onset stroke as log-odds. A)
    Base model. B) Adjused for sociodemographic characteristics. C) Adjused for Clinical characteristics. D) Missingness of Wake-up status E) health trust.
    F) Full model.}
  \label{fig:adjusted_maps}
\end{figure}

The municipality level estimates were practically unchanged when adjusted for
the patient-related sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Compared to
the base model, the municipality level log-odds estimes changed with median of
0.00 (IQR -0.02 - 0.02) for the sociodemographic model and -0.01 (IQR -0.03 -
0.03) for the clinical model (figure \ref{fig:adjusted_maps}B-C). Adjustment
for missing wake-up status produced a wider distribution of changes (median
-0.01, IQR -0.08 - 0.08) (figure \ref{fig:adjusted_maps}D). In contrast to the
minimal visual changes seen after sociodemographic, clinical and missingness
adjustment, the health trust (-0.08 median, IQR -0.48 - 0.48) and full models
(-0.06 median, IQR -0.51 - 0.49) produced noticable change in the the sptial
pattern (figure \ref{fig:adjusted_maps} E-F).

Changes in the municipality-level variation were reflected in the posterior
standard deviation (SD) of the spatial effect. Compared to the base model (SD
0.696), the posterior standard deviation of the municipality effect of the
sociodemographic (SD 0.697) and clinical models (SD 0.697) were unchanged.
While higher spatial sd was observed for the missingness model (SD 0.775),
lower spatial SD was obtained for the health trust model (SD 0.518) and full
models (SD 0.612). The spatial mixing parameter remained high and largely
unchanged across models, ranging from 0.969 to 0.976, indicating that the
residual municipality-level variation remained predominantly spatially
structured (table \ref{tab:spatial_models}).

The DIC and WAIC were lower for all models compared to the base model
indicating improved model fit. The additoin of patietn-related factors improved
the model fit without substantially altering the spatial pattern. Among the
individuall adjustment models, the largest reduction was observed for the
missingness model (DIC 54270.76, WAIC 54269.53), accompanied with increase in
the municipallity level sd. Inclusion of health trust produced a smaller
improvement in model fit (DIC, 60,710.35; WAIC, 60,710.54) but was accompanied
by a marked reduction in spatial variation. The full model which included all
patient and provider factors had the lowest DIC (53,638.95) and WAIC
(53,640.24).
